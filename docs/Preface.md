# Preface

Never before have the findings of archaeology been linked so extensively with the Scriptures as in The Reader's Digest illustrated edition of the Holy Bible. It contains more than five hundred illustrations and captions that bring the background of history and the insights of archaeology to Biblical narratives and themes.

This edition became possible in 1961, when scholars completed a six-year project of compiling and describing photographs of ancient artifacts, monuments, inscriptions, works of art, and archaeological sites to illustrate -- that is "to set in a clear light" -- innumerable subjects in the Bible. With this vast materials before them, the editors of The Reader's Digest have worked for many years to develop and publish The Reader's Digest illustrated edition of the Holy Bible.

Until the preparation of this book, illustrations for the Bible were usually drawn from an artist's imagination -- more often than not from the imagery of an artist totally unfamiliar with the [ancient Near East](wiki:Ancient_Near_East). Although reverent and beautiful as expressions of faith, these paintings and drawings have frequently given an inaccurate picture of the Biblical world. Now the archaeological photographs in The Reader's Digest illustrated edition of the Holy Bible show what much of that world was like.

The story of how Biblical archaeology developed is fascinating. From the fifteenth to the nineteenth century many Christian scholars traveled in [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) and later wrote about the place they visited, but in 1838 a revolution in the exploration of the [Holy Land](wiki:Holy_Land) occurred. During three months of that year, the American theologian [Edward Robnison](wiki:Edward_Robinson_(scholar)) journeyed through almost the entire area, and with the help of [Eli Smith](wiki:Eli_Smith), a fellow American who spoke fluent Arabic, correctly identified for the first time scores of ancient site whose true Biblical identities he deduced from their modern Arabic names. Another famous nineteenth century explorer of the Holy Land was the young French scholar [Charles Clemont-Ganneau](wiki:Charles_Simon_Clermont-Ganneau). In 1870, he recovered from the Arabs the famous [Moabite Stone](wiki:Mesha_Stele) (see {ref}`here<Mesha_Stele>`).

The first archaeologist systematically to excavate a Palestinian "[tell](wiki:Tell_(archaeology))" -- a large artificial mound formed from the debris of ancient human settlements -- was an Englishman named [Flinders Petrie](wiki:Flinders_Petrie). (The [great tell of Jericho](wiki:Tell_es-Sultan) is shown here; that of the fortress-city [Beth-shan](wiki:Beit_She'an) is shown here). [Petrie](wiki:Flinders_Petrie) recognized that when men live on a specific site over a long period of time, the successive building, destruction, and rebuilding of houses and the deposition of waste materials increase the height of the land on which the settlement is located by accumulating layer upon layer of trash and rubble -- the earliest at the bottom and the most recent on or just below the surface. In 1890 he began the excavation of [Tell el-Hesi](wiki:Tell_el-Hesi), a high mound in southwestern [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)). By cutting trenches from top to bottom on the sides of the [tell](wiki:Tell_(archaeology)) -- an operation somewhat like cutting slices off the side of a gigantic layer cake -- he was able to distinguish the different strata of debris that had accumulated during the times when the site was inhabited, and to examine and categorize the distinctive types of pottery found in each stratum. The method of pottery analysis initiated by [Petrie](wiki:Flinders_Petrie) and refined by later archaeologists makes it possible to assign quite accurate dates to artifacts found in association with certain kinds of potsherds, or pottery fragments.

The techniques of archaeological excavation have improved greatly during the twentieth century, and particularly since [World War II](wiki:World_War_II). One method of digging that has proved widely successful in the [Near East](wiki:Near_East) is the so-called grid system -- introduced to [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) by [Kathleen Kenyon](wiki:Kathleen_Kenyon), an English archaeologist who pioneered its use on prehistoric British sites. The site to be explored is laid out in squares of about twenty feet on a side that are separated from one another by intervals two or three feet wide, forming a vast grid. The squares are then excavated, with any discovered structure left infact, and the areas between the squares are left standing as walls. Objects found in the excavated squares can be associated with the bands of strata visible in the adjacent walls and dated accordingly.

The most recent major excavation of Biblical site took place during 1963-1965, when a large group of volunteer workers under the direction of [Yigael Yadin](wiki:Yigael_Yadin) cleared the fortress-palace that [Herod the Great](wiki:Herod_the_Great) had built at [Masada](wiki:Masada), a high [mesa](wiki:Mesa) to the southwest of the [Dead Sea](wiki:Dead_Sea) (see {ref}`here<fig:Masada_Aerial_south_view>`).

Other important archaeological excavations illustrated in this book include those at the [city of Megiddo](wiki:Megiddo,_Israel) (also see here, 1st Kings, chapter 9) and at [Hazor](wiki:Tel_Hazor), a city in ortheastern [Galilee](wiki:Galilee). At both places archaeologists uncovered several rows of stables for chariot horses, built by [King Ahab](wiki:Ahab) (also see here, 1st Kings, chapter 20). At these two cities and also at [Gezer](wiki:Gezer), gates and city walls were discovered that had been constructed by [Solomon](wiki:Solomon) or [Ahab](wiki:Ahab) or both (also see here, 1st Kings, chapter 20).

The Reader's Digest illustrated edition of the Holy Bible contains photographs of these and many other place in modern [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) that have remained essentially unchanged since Biblical times. For example, there are pictures of the site of ancient [Anathoth](wiki:Anthoth), the home of the prophet [Jeremiah](wiki:Jeremiah) (see here, Jeremiah chapter 1); the great stones of the wall that bounded [the Temple](wiki:Temple_in_Jerusalem) at [Jerusalem](wiki:Jerusalem) in the time of {ref}`Jesus<WHO_Jesus>` (see here, Mark 13); the [Negeb](wiki:Negev), where the apostle {ref}`Philip<WHO_Philip>` won a convert to Christianity (see here, Acts 8); the remains of cities {ref}`Paul<WHO_Paul>` visited on his missionary journeys (see here (Acts 13), here (Acts 16), and here (Acts 18)).

Prominent among the inscriptions found in [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) that illustrate Biblical events is the famour [Siloam Inscription](wiki:Siloam_inscription) (see {ref}`here<fig:Siloam_Inscription>`), which [King Hezekiah](wiki:Hezekiah)'s workmen cut in rock after digging the underground tunnel that brought water to [Jerusalem's Pool of Siloam](wiki:Pool_of_Siloam) (see {ref}`here<fig:Hezekiah's_Tunnel>` and {ref}`here<fig:Pool_of_Siloam>`). from the [Spring of Gihon](wiki:Gihon_Spring). Another outstanding inscription was found by excavators at [Caesarea](wiki:Caesarea_Maritima): a stone inscribed with the name of [Pontius Pilate](wiki:Pontius_Pilate) (see {ref}`here<fig:Pilate_Inscription>`), the only such inscription yet found.

Among the best-known Biblical writings discovered in [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) are the so-called [Dead Sea Scrolls](wiki:Dead_Sea_Scrolls). In 1947 an Arab goatherd found the first of these scrolls in a cave near [Qumran](wiki:Qumran), on a cliff overlooking the northwestern shore of the [Dead Sea](wiki:Dead_Sea). For a reproduction of part of one of these scrolls containing text of the Book of Isaiah, see {ref}`here<fig:Dead_Sea_Scroll_Isaiah>`.

Archaeologists -- and enterprising [Bedouin](wiki:Bedouin) tribesmen who had learned that they would be well paid for such finds -- soon discovered fragments of many more Hebrew scrolls in a number of caves in the vicinity of [Qumran](wiki:Qumran). Scholars believe that the scrolls were probably left by the member of a Jewish religious sect known as the [Essenes](wiki:Essenes), who had established a monastic center at [Qumran](wiki:Qumran) in the century before the birth of {ref}`Jesus<WHO_Jesus>`. In one of the caves, which must have served as the library of the religious community, thousands of fragments from hundreds of different manuscripts were discovered. At least part of every book of the Old Testament except Esther have been found.

It is not only the [Holy Land](wiki:Holy_Land) that contained many ancient artifacts and inscriptions relating to the bible. The neighboring lands of [Syria](wiki:Syria) to the north and especially [Mesopotamia](wiki:Mesopotamia) to the east and [Egypt](wiki:Egypt) to the southwest of [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)) also have yielded abundant material casting light on many Biblical passages. For instance, the Babylonian [Code of Hammurabi](wiki:Code_of_Hammurabi) (see {ref}`here<fig:Code_Hammurabi>`) contains several laws quite similiar to [Mosaic laws](wiki:Law_of_Moses) found in the [Pentateuch](wiki:Torah), the first five books of the Bible. Inscriptions and reliefs of the Kings of [Assyria](wiki:Assyria) from the eighth and seventh centries B.c. (see {ref}`here<fig:Assyrian_Batteringram>`, {ref}`here<fig:Black_Obelist_Yehu>`, and {ref}`here<fig:Tilglath_pileser_III>`) help bring alive many events mentioned in the historical and prophetic books of the Old Testament.

Works of art -- sculptures, paintings, and smaller objects -- from two great cultural centers of the ancient world, [Mesopotamia](wiki:Mesopotamia) and [Egypt](wiki:Ancient_Egypt), are also shown in this book. The [Law of Moses](wiki:Law_of_Moses) forbade the Jews to make graven images -- and, as a result, there are no direct representations of life in the [Holy Land](wiki:Holy_Land) that have been left us by Jewish artists. But because the material cultures of the [Near East](wiki:Near_East) during Bible times were so much alike, artifacts unearthed in these other countries illustrate with considerably accuracy dress, customs, and occupations as they existed in [Palestine](wiki:Palestine_(region)). All the artifacts shown in The Reader's Digest illustrated edition of the Holy Bible have been selected and arranged to correspond as closely as possible to Biblical chronology.
